How To Build A Character Audiences Actually Care About

JUJU Editorial — June 2026 — 12 min read

Audiences do not care about characters because they are likable. They care because something about the character still feels unresolved.

That unresolved quality creates curiosity, tension, and the feeling that something still matters. Without it, a character can be entertaining and still be forgettable.

01 — The Core Problem

Why Audiences Follow Some Characters And Forget Others

The gap between a memorable character and a meaningful one is larger than most creators realise. Many characters are easy to remember — they have a distinctive look, a sharp sense of humour, or a strong personality. Those qualities help them stand out for a moment. They do not automatically make people care.

Audiences become attached when they feel that a character is in the middle of something. A difficult decision. A personal conflict. A relationship that could change their future. A goal that feels uncertain. That is usually the point where viewing becomes emotional rather than observational.

"The audience is not watching to see what happens. They are watching because they are afraid of what might happen — and they need to know."

The characters audiences tend to forget are the ones who never seem to be at risk of anything. They are competent, confident, and undisturbed. Nothing meaningful presses against them. They go through the plot without going through anything personal.

02 — The Mechanism

Desire And Obstacle Create The Engine

The simplest version of what makes a character work is this: they want something, and something is in the way. That structure is not a formula. It is a description of how narrative tension actually functions.

The want does not need to be dramatic. It can be quiet — the desire to be understood, to not repeat a mistake, to hold a family together, to admit something that has been hidden. What matters is that the want is real and that the obstacle is genuinely difficult.

What creates audience investment
  • A character who wants something specific they cannot easily get
  • An obstacle that reveals something true about who the character is
  • A cost — something the character risks by trying to get what they want
  • A gap between how the character sees themselves and what the audience can see
  • A decision the audience is not sure the character will make correctly

Without cost, the story has no pressure. Without pressure, the audience has no reason to worry. And if the audience is not worried, they are not truly invested.

03 — Craft Detail

Why Flaws Work When Sympathy Alone Does Not

Sympathy is easy to manufacture. Give a character a difficult past, a loss, or an unfair situation and most audiences will feel something for them. That feeling rarely lasts on its own.

Flaws create a different kind of connection. A flaw is not simply a weakness — it is a pattern of behaviour that costs the character something and that they cannot fully see or control. It is the engine of most meaningful character arcs.

  1. Inconsistency Under PressureA character who behaves consistently in comfort but inconsistently under pressure feels human. The inconsistency is not a writing problem — it is the information the audience is waiting for.
  2. Self-DeceptionCharacters who misread their own motivations create dramatic irony. The audience understands something the character does not. That gap creates tension that almost no other technique can replicate.
  3. The Wrong SolutionA character who solves the right problem in the wrong way is more compelling than one who always finds the correct answer. The wrong solution reveals values, priorities, and blind spots.
  4. A Cost The Character Keeps PayingIf a flaw has no ongoing consequences, it is not really a flaw — it is a personality note. A real flaw shows up repeatedly, costs something real, and resists easy resolution.
04 — Applied To VMD

Building Character In Vertical Micro Dramas

Vertical micro dramas have a structural challenge that longer formats do not: the entire first episode must establish enough about a character that a viewer feels something before they decide whether to continue.

The audience is not committed. They are auditing. They will decide in thirty seconds whether this character is worth their attention. That means the opening has to do something different from what most long-form drama does.

"In VMD, character work is not scene-building — it is signal compression. Every line of dialogue, every reaction, every visual choice has to do the work that a feature film might spread across twenty minutes."

The most effective VMD characters are usually introduced mid-problem. Not at the start of a situation — in the middle of it, already making a choice, already in conflict with something or someone. That compression removes the setup phase and drops the audience directly into the emotional stakes.

VMD character entry techniques
  • Open on a decision already in progress, not a situation being established
  • Give the character one line that implies a full history without explaining it
  • Use the physical environment to communicate something the character has not said
  • Introduce a contradiction in the first thirty seconds — something that does not quite add up
  • Let the character want something the audience can immediately understand
05 — The Arc

Change, Resistance, And What Actually Moves An Audience

A character arc is usually described as change — the character starts in one place and ends in another. That description is incomplete. What actually moves an audience is not the change itself but the resistance to change.

A character who changes easily is not interesting. A character who fights against change, who has everything to lose by becoming different, who changes only because they have no other option left — that is where most of the emotion lives.

Even flat arcs work when the character's refusal to change creates pressure on everything around them. The world changes, other characters change, and the static character becomes a kind of friction. That friction is narratively productive when it is handled deliberately.

The goal is not to make a character likable. The goal is to make the audience feel that the character's situation is not finished yet — that something is still unresolved, still pressing, still at risk. That feeling, more than any single technique, is what keeps people watching.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What makes a character feel relatable?
A character feels relatable when the audience recognises an emotion, conflict, or insecurity they understand. Relatability usually comes from emotional truth, not from similarity in lifestyle or personality.
Do characters need to be likable?
No. Likability helps in some stories, but it is not the main driver of audience care. Audiences often connect more strongly with characters who are flawed, complicated, or still working through something.
Why do audiences connect with flawed characters?
Flaws create vulnerability, pressure, and contradiction. Those qualities make a character feel human and give the audience something to understand, anticipate, or worry about.
What makes a protagonist memorable?
A memorable protagonist usually wants something badly, behaves in a way that reveals character under pressure, and changes in a way the audience can feel.
Why do some characters feel flat?
Characters often feel flat when they are too perfect, too explained, too static, or too free of conflict. When nothing feels unfinished, the audience has less reason to stay engaged.
